The best performer isn't always the best manager

Developing first-line and middle managers: where should L&D start?

Hua Minh Quan · October 2026 · 11 min read
  • This article draws on academic research, reports from research institutes and management consultancies, and the author's observations from delivering training projects with organizations. Names and personal details have been changed.
  • Sources are numbered and listed at the end of the article.
  • AI tools were used for data synthesis, editing and publishing.

Following on from my article on approaching training by starting from the business problem rather than from individual courses, this one takes a more specific slice: addressing the development needs of middle managers. It starts by locating the problem in mindset and core capabilities, then moves on to the idea of building a competency framework from FranklinCovey's 6 Critical Practices model.

Josh's story

Josh led his region in sales for three years running as a sales rep at a company that manufactures and supplies materials for the furniture industry. Having previously worked for a company in the same industry, he understood customer behavior, knew every dealer well and rarely missed his individual targets. He quickly stepped into the vacant Sales Lead role on the strength of his hands-on profile and track record.

Six months later, the team's sales were starting to slide, and two of the seven team members had left. And at the end of every month, Josh was still out in the market closing deals on behalf of his reps: “It's faster if I do it myself. If I leave it to them, we won't make it.”

The company lost a great salesperson and gained a manager who was struggling.

I came across similar cases regularly in my work with corporate clients as a training consultant, and the pattern is backed by more objective data. Economists Alan Benson, Danielle Li and Kelly Shue analyzed data on nearly 40,000 sales workers at 131 firms. On promotions, they found that a worker whose sales were twice as high as a colleague's was about 15% more likely to be promoted in that month, yet the teams of these managers went on to perform worse than those led by managers who had been weaker salespeople [1]. Published in The Quarterly Journal of Economics, the study is considered quantitative evidence for the “Peter Principle”: people tend to be promoted until they reach a position in which they no longer perform well. Put slightly differently, doing well in an individual role does not prove someone can do well in a leadership role; management calls for a new set of capabilities.

The story repeats itself across organizations in different settings: a skilled worker becomes a team leader or line leader, a senior specialist with deep expertise is promoted to head of department, a highly experienced developer becomes the lead of a digital transformation and AI project. They are being asked to do a completely different job without structured preparation, and both their own effectiveness and that of the teams they lead decline as a result.

The most important link is also the one under the most pressure

Before going further, it is worth being clear about who this article covers. I group the two populations below together because they share certain features of the same transition:

  • First-line managers: directly manage individual contributors, for example team leaders, shift leaders, Sales Leads and Team Leads.
  • Middle managers: manage other managers, for example Area Sales Managers (ASMs) managing Sales Leads, or production managers managing shift leaders.

These two groups are where leadership's strategy turns into the team's day-to-day work. And they are under enormous pressure.

A Gartner survey conducted in July 2024 found that three in four of the 805 HR leaders surveyed said their managers were overwhelmed by their growing responsibilities, while leader and manager development remained HR leaders' top priority for 2025, for the third consecutive year [3].

Promoting strong specialists into management is a sensible and common practice worldwide: they understand the work, have credibility with their peers and have demonstrated commitment. The difficulty lies not in the promotion decision itself, but in what happens around it. In my observation, three layers of factors are intertwined.

Layer 1 – The individual: mindset makes or breaks it

Linda Hill, a professor at Harvard Business School, studied new managers during their first year in the role. She describes the first test of a new manager's leadership as learning how to manage others effectively. According to Hill, new managers tend to focus on the rights and privileges that come with being the boss, but instead of gaining new power, they find themselves hemmed in by constraints and by their dependence on many intertwined organizational factors [4].

I believe the hardest shift is not learning new skills. The hardest part is changing how you define your own success: from “I succeed when I personally create and prove results” to “I only succeed when my team succeeds”. Under pressure, people tend to fall back on what they do best. That is why Josh kept rushing out to close deals at the end of every month.

Layer 2 – Operating mechanisms: sensible designs that don't fit together

Most people policies are designed with good intentions. But when certain mechanisms sit side by side, they can unintentionally work against new managers.

  • Individual performance is the easiest criterion to measure, but good managers need more than technical excellence. When a promotion decision has to be made, sales figures or productivity are the clearest, cheapest, fastest and fairest data at hand. Benson and colleagues found this to be true in most of the firms they studied, and observed that firms do not tend to prioritize promoting employees with more teamwork experience, even though those employees typically go on to become better managers [1].
  • The “player-coach” role. Many first-line management roles still carry individual KPIs alongside responsibility for the team's targets, which often makes business sense. A McKinsey survey of 706 middle managers found that they spend nearly half of their time on non-managerial work and less than a third on managing talent and people [5]. When both roles compete for time, the one with immediately measurable results (KPIs) usually wins. The team's results then end up serving short-term, reactive goals rather than long-term, sustainable outcomes.
  • Managers' own managers are overloaded too. The layers above have yet to prioritize resources (time, effort, people and budget) for coaching and developing the managers below them and their own future successors. When they are themselves overloaded with individual targets, coaching easily gets pushed behind more urgent work.
  • Succession pipelines take time. A well-structured succession plan prepares candidates before they step into the role. It is a long-term investment that many organizations are still building. I will explore this topic in a separate article.

Layer 3 – Implementation conditions: resources and the ability to prove impact

Even when an organization has clearly identified the problem, developing a new generation of managers still means balancing three conditions: budget, time and the ability to measure impact. I discuss this further in my article on the three challenges facing L&D.

Why start with a competency framework: what does a good manager look like in our organization?

Given the overall picture:

  • Individual performance is almost the only basis for promotion.
  • Development programs easily turn into a list of disconnected soft-skills courses.
  • There is no shared standard for measuring change.

A competency framework is a structured answer to that question, the thread that connects promotion, development and evaluation. If, as Gallup estimates, only about one in ten people has high talent to manage [2], then selection matters as much as training. In other words, the competency framework should be used from the selection stage onward, not only as an input for course design.

But a competency framework is only useful when it is written in observable, everyday behaviors, rather than generic adjectives such as “visionary” or “good communicator”.

A proposed approach

I chose FranklinCovey's The 6 Critical Practices for Leading a Team® (6CP) [6] as the backbone of a thorough solution to this problem. Three examples drawn from practice show the diversity of the learner groups:

  • FMCG: mainly sales management teams.
  • F&B: the entire middle management team across all departments.
  • Manufacturing and light industry: production managers and heads of the plant's functional departments.

The observations below therefore come from hands-on experience, and readers should weigh them with that perspective in mind.

There are three reasons I believe 6CP fits the problem discussed in this article:

  1. It starts at the real bottleneck. The model's premise is exactly the mindset shift described in Layer 1: from individual contributor to someone who achieves results through the team, not at the expense of the team.
  2. It is written as habits, not abstract competencies. Each practice is a set of behaviors managers can put into action within the week, which makes them easy to turn into observation and measurement criteria.
  3. It balances results and people. Managers are expected to set high standards while genuinely caring about their team, rather than having to choose one over the other.

For management groups with heavy workloads, target pressure and constant decisions to make, the first question they ask is usually: “What will I do differently with my team next Monday?” 6CP answers that question directly, which is why learners readily embrace it.

Below is how I interpret the six practices and map them to the bottlenecks analyzed above:

PracticeIn shortBottleneck addressed
1. Align Purpose & PerformanceHelp each person see how their work connects to shared goalsThe “bridge” role that carries strategy down to the team
2. Communicate as a CoachAsk more than direct, so others find their own solutionsThe habit of “doing it myself because it's faster”
3. Delegate to ElevateDelegate according to each person's capability and readiness, with the right level of supportThe “player-coach” role
4. Use Feedback as FuelGive frequent, specific, forward-looking feedback, and actively ask for itNo mechanism for continuous learning within the team
5. Gain, Don't Drain, EnergyManage one's own energy as a strategic resource and model it for the teamBurned-out, disengaged managers
6. Leverage 1-on-1s to WinHold regular 1-on-1s, with accountability on both sides, tailored to each personNo regular channel for coaching and connection

Two notes on application:

  • For middle managers, the six practices stay the same, but the audience changes: they practice them with their own first-line managers. A major advantage is that both levels share the same language, so middle managers can coach their direct reports on exactly what those reports are learning.
  • The model needs to be “translated” into the local context. For example, for an FMCG Sales Lead, practices 2 and 3 could be described as going into the field with sales reps at least [X] times a week to observe and give feedback.

From competency framework to action: four steps

Step 1 – Define

Use the six practices as the framework, then translate them into observable behaviors for each role across three layers: Mindset – Skillset – Toolset. This is best done together with senior leaders and with the managers who are already performing well in the organization.

Step 2 – Select

Build the competency framework into promotion criteria. Beyond individual performance, assess management potential.

Just as important is a genuine desire to manage. Not every high performer wants to become a manager. Some organizations address this with dual career ladders (split career ladders) [1].

Step 3 – Develop

To address time and budget constraints, I apply the spirit of the 70-20-10 model, which originated in research at the Center for Creative Leadership [7]: most of a manager's learning happens through on-the-job experience, some through coaching and feedback, and a small part through formal learning. Note that this is a design principle, not a validated ratio:

  • Formal learning (10): short modules, each focused on one practice, so managers do not have to leave their posts for days at a time.
  • Coaching (20): direct supervisors use practice 6 (1-on-1s) and practice 4 (feedback) to coach new managers.
  • Experience (70): after each module, managers apply that practice with their team for a few weeks, tied to the team's own KPIs, before moving on to the next practice.

Step 4 – Measure

Use the Kirkpatrick model's four levels: Reaction → Learning → Behavior → Results [8]. Most programs stop at Levels 1 and 2, measured through field notes, end-of-course surveys and tests. A well-defined competency framework makes Level 3 much easier to measure: Do managers hold regular 1-on-1s? How has the share of work they delegate, rather than do themselves, changed? How does the team rate the quality of their feedback? From there, connect to Level 4 (team turnover, engagement, business results). This is how to answer the question “is the investment paying off?”, which, as Gartner shows, many organizations are struggling with [3].

Closing thoughts

Promoting a high performer into management is an investment. Without preparation, the organization may gain a good manager, but it may also lose both a strong performer and a team.

A competency framework does not solve everything. But it turns the question “can this person manage?” from gut feeling into an evidence-based conversation, and gives people-development professionals an anchor for designing, delivering and proving value.
