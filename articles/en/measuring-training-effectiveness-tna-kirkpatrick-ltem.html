Measuring training effectiveness: from needs analysis to Kirkpatrick and LTEM

Before you measure, diagnose

Hua Minh Quan · October 2026 · 9 min read
  • This article draws on academic research, reports from research institutes and management consultancies, and the author's observations from delivering training projects with organizations. Names and personal details have been changed.
  • Sources are numbered and listed at the end of the article.
  • AI tools were used for data synthesis, editing and publishing.

In the previous article, I outlined three challenges that L&D teams often have to tackle at the same time: budget, measuring effectiveness, and workload. This article focuses on the second one: measurement. But rather than opening with evaluation models, it takes a step back to training needs analysis, because most of what can be measured after a course has already been decided before the course begins. In short: before you measure, diagnose.

Measurement starts before the course

A program can only be called effective if we know exactly what it was designed to change. It sounds obvious, yet it is easily overlooked when planning evaluation. Why? Because in practice, organizations can easily end up in situations where learning objectives and evaluation criteria are written after the course has already been chosen (training requests that “pop up” from a department or the board are one example).

Jack and Patti Phillips's ROI Methodology includes a principle that I think is well worth keeping in mind: without baseline data, assume no improvement has occurred [1]. In other words, measurement actually begins at the needs analysis stage, when the L&D team captures the current state before any intervention, through training or otherwise, takes place.

The four types of needs in Dana Gaines Robinson's performance consulting model, discussed in the previous article, also serve as a map for measurement [2]: business needs tell you which results to track; performance needs tell you which on-the-job behaviors to observe; individual learning needs tell you what people must know and be able to do; and organizational conditions tell you what may block application, even when people have learned well.

Needs analysis: more than a survey

In many organizations, training needs analysis (TNA) is understood as a survey sent to departments at the end of the year, asking what they would like to learn. CIPD proposes a broader view through learning needs analysis (LNA): an ongoing “health check” of people capability across the organization, rather than a one-off exercise before a course is launched [3]. According to CIPD, analysis should take place at three levels, viewed in relation to one another:

  • Organization: strategy, business goals and major upcoming changes.
  • Department or project: performance requirements for each team responsible for a specific area of work.
  • Individual: the gap between current capability and what the role requires.

Nor should the data come from a single source. CIPD lists many channels that can be combined: organizational data; interviews and group discussions; informal conversations with stakeholders; observation of team meetings; surveys; performance data; competency frameworks; and AI-assisted analysis. It adds two notable recommendations: prioritize the capabilities that affect business performance, and always consider solutions other than running a course [3].

When skills data is thin, in both volume and depth

Many organizations want to invest in learning and development without a clear view of their own people's capabilities. A 2026 survey by The Harris Poll for the University of Phoenix found that only 43% of HR leaders are confident their employees have the skills needed for an increasingly automated workplace, and fewer than half believe their managers are equipped to identify and assess skill gaps together with employees. Skill gaps are identified mainly through manager assessments and periodic performance reviews [4].

Earlier, McKinsey recommended that the first step of any reskilling strategy is a systematic diagnosis: comparing the supply of each existing skill with the business's strategic needs, role group by role group, before deciding whether to build or hire [5]. Its 2019 survey found that 87% of organizations were facing or expected to face skill gaps, yet only 33% felt prepared to address them [5].

When data comes mainly from managers' assessments, and managers themselves are unsure how to assess, LNA/TNA has to triangulate across multiple sources. This is where an L&D professional's research and consulting skills show most clearly: asking the right questions, hearing what is left unsaid, and pulling scattered pieces of information together into an insight solid enough to act on.

In the next article, on building capability for middle managers, I look at how to build a competency framework, which in turn supports planning how to measure and close capability gaps for a specific role. The framework draws on FranklinCovey's The 6 Critical Practices for Leading a Team®, which I suggest extending into a concrete competency “dictionary” for managers, serving as the basis for measuring before and after the learning journey.

From needs to measures

Once needs are clear at each level, the evaluation plan can be sketched out during the analysis itself. The table below suggests how to link each type of need to the questions to answer during TNA/LNA and to what should be measured after training:

Kirkpatrick: the profession's common language

The most familiar tool for training professionals is Kirkpatrick's four-level model: Reaction → Learning → Behavior → Results [6].

  • Strengths: it is simple, easy to explain to leaders, and has become the profession's common language. Say “this program only measures up to Level 2” and anyone in the field immediately sees where the gap is.
  • Limitations: most programs stop at Levels 1 and 2, meaning end-of-course surveys and tests. The model is also often read as an automatic causal chain: satisfied learners will learn, and people who learn will behave differently (an assumption that, in practice, has many gaps and rarely produces convincing evidence). In addition, Level 2 “Learning” lumps together very different things, from recalling knowledge to being able to perform a task. That is why I see Kirkpatrick's levels as more of a reference point than a tool to apply in practice.

LTEM: a deeper approach focused on real-world application

Will Thalheimer, a learning researcher, published the Learning-Transfer Evaluation Model (LTEM) in 2018 as an alternative to Kirkpatrick. He argues that the four-level model has “done a tremendous amount of harm” by leading training professionals to settle for shallow evaluation [7]. LTEM has eight tiers:

  • Tiers 1–2 – Attendance and Activity: learners show up and take part.
  • Tier 3 – Learner Perceptions.
  • Tier 4 – Knowledge.
  • Tier 5 – Decision-Making Competence: knowing what to do in a realistic situation.
  • Tier 6 – Task Competence: being able to do it.
  • Tier 7 – Transfer: applying it in real work.
  • Tier 8 – Effects of Transfer: on learners, teams and the organization.

According to Thalheimer, the first four tiers are not enough to show that learning is effective, yet most organizations still stop at Tiers 3–4: satisfaction surveys and knowledge tests [7]. He also recommends measuring a few days after the session rather than at the end of class, to see what learners actually remember.

LTEM's most useful feature for L&D practitioners is that Tiers 5 and 6 are within easy reach. For example, after a coaching skills course for managers, instead of only handing out a survey, learners can work through a simulated one-on-one scenario one to two weeks later. This does not yet require KPI data from other departments, but it already tells you more than a satisfaction score.

Tier 7 is where many programs lose momentum when it comes to measurement. According to CIPD, only 8% of L&D teams prioritize learning transfer, meaning applying what has been learned in everyday work [8]. This is also where the “organizational conditions” row in the table above earns its place: if the TNA has shown that line managers are not supportive, or that processes do not allow people to work differently, L&D knows in advance that Tier 7 will be hard to reach and can prepare from the outset.

Closing thoughts

No evaluation model is of much use when a program has defined the wrong problem from the start. Kirkpatrick, LTEM or even ROI only work when L&D knows exactly what needs to change, for whom, and where things stand today. That is why the time most worth investing in measurement usually goes into needs analysis.
